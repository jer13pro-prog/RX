How recruiting actually works

Coaches see everyone. They call the athletes who make it easy.

A college distance coach can pull up every 1600 and 5K time in California tonight. Seeing your athlete was never the hard part. The hard part is what comes after: with hundreds of names behind every results page and a handful of roster spots to fill, no coach can evaluate, contact, and build a relationship with every kid who ran well.

So they work the way any busy person works — down the path of least resistance. When a fast athlete lands in their inbox with verified FAT times, race film, transcripts, a clear event fit, and a specific reason the program makes sense, the coach's job just got easier. That athlete moves up the list. The athlete whose marks sit untouched in a database, or whose one email was missing half of what the coach needed, stays where they are.

Fast makes your athlete recruitable. Organized is what gets them recruited.

And there's a second half almost nobody manages: what happens when the interest comes. The call from a coach, the visit invite, the "we'd love to have you" that may or may not be an actual offer. Families walk into those moments blind — I know, because I did.

Who I am

The calls came. I didn't know what to do with them.

My name is Jacob Resendez. I grew up in Bakersfield, graduated from Liberty High in 2019, and ran 16:20 for the 5K. Coaches did reach out — and I had no idea what I was listening to. I didn't know what their questions meant, what I should be asking back, how a walk-on invitation differed from a real offer, or what "stay in touch" actually signals. Nobody in my corner had been through it. I made my decision mostly on feel.

I got lucky: Fresno Pacific turned out to be exactly the right fit. Over four years there I went from 16:20 to 14:09 in the 5000, from 4:23 to 3:48 in the 1500, and finished runner-up in the 1500 at the PacWest Conference Championships. I lived every stage of the four-year climb — which is the thing parents are really buying when they hire a coach.

After graduating, I stayed at FPU as Interim Head Cross Country Coach, recruiting athletes myself and reading the same inbox I'd once been trying to reach.

Program credential

I coached at Fresno Pacific during the program's historic 2025 season — the women's team finished 4th at the NCAA Division II Outdoor National Championships, the highest finish in school history, headlined by a 4x100m relay national title and an individual 400m hurdles national championship. I saw what a national-caliber program looks like from the inside — how it trains, how it recruits, and what its coaches look for.

So that's the full loop: the high school kid fielding calls he didn't understand, the college athlete who developed into conference-level marks, and the college coach making recruiting decisions. Every family I work with gets all three.

Questions parents ask

Straight answers

My kid's times might not be good enough for college.

Most families judge their athlete against the fastest kids they've personally watched — but college rosters span a huge range across D1, D2, D3, and NAIA, and coaches weigh trajectory and coachability alongside the number. The College Match Report exists to answer this honestly before you spend a dollar. If college running isn't realistic, I'll tell you that too.

We already emailed some coaches and heard nothing.

Usually the email was missing something the coach needed to act on it — verified FAT marks, film, academics, event fit — or it simply never got a follow-up. Silence after one message tells you almost nothing. That's a fixable problem, not a verdict on your athlete.

What if a coach calls and we don't know what to say?

That's exactly the situation I build families for, because I lived the opposite of it. Before any call, your athlete knows what the coach's questions are really measuring, what to ask back, and what a genuine offer sounds like versus polite interest. Nobody walks into those conversations alone.

My athlete already has a high school or club coach.

Great — keep them. The Recruiting Campaign exists for exactly that situation: your current coach handles training, I handle the target list, the outreach, the follow-up, and the offer process, and I coordinate so the race schedule and the recruiting timeline work together instead of against each other.

Why only distance runners?

Because it's what I know at a professional level. I raced 800 through 5K and cross country in college, and I coached it. Sprinters and field-event athletes deserve someone with the same depth in their events — that isn't me, and I won't pretend it is.

When should we start?

Sophomore or junior year is ideal — coaches build their boards early, and rosters fill well before signing day. Seniors still have real options, but the timeline is tighter and the execution has to be sharper. Either way, the sooner your athlete is easy to say yes to, the more choices you'll have.
